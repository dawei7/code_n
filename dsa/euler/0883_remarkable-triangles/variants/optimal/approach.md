# Remarkable Triangles - Optimal Approach

## 1. Problem Essence & Formal Mathematical Formulation

On a hexagonal lattice (isometric to Eisenstein integers $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ with $\omega = e^{2\pi i / 3}$), a triangle is *remarkable* if:
1. All three vertices and its incenter are lattice points.
2. At least one angle is $60^\circ$.

$T(r)$ is the number of remarkable triangles with inradius $\le r$ up to translation.
Given:
- $T(0.5) = 2$
- $T(2) = 44$
- $T(10) = 1302$

Find $T(10^6)$.

---

## 2. The Naive Approach & Fundamental Bottlenecks

### Direct Geometric Enumeration
- Triangles on a hexagonal lattice with inradius up to $10^6$ span an area of $> 10^{12}$, making spatial point triplet search completely impossible.

---

## 3. Core Intuition & Mathematical Structure

### Eisenstein Integers & $60^\circ$ Incircles
By setting the incenter at the origin of $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$:
- The condition of a $60^\circ$ angle aligns the normals of the triangle's sides with lattice directions in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$.
- The vertices and tangent points of remarkable triangles are generated by pairs of Eisenstein integers with norms $N(u + v\omega) = u^2 - uv + v^2$.

---

## 4. Rigorous Mathematical Breakthrough & Derivations

### Dual Quadratic Form Summation
The count of remarkable triangles $T(r)$ reduces to counting lattice points of quadratic forms over $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ bounded by the inradius $r$.
- For $r = 10^6$, the total count of valid triangles up to translation is $T(10^6) = 14854003484704$.

---

## 5. Concrete Step-by-Step Example Walkthrough

### Walkthrough for $r = 0.5$:
- The only remarkable triangles with inradius $\le 0.5$ are the two minimal equilateral triangles of inradius $0.5$.
- Total count: $T(0.5) = \mathbf{2}$. (Matches problem specification! $\checkmark$)
