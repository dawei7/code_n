# Guided Example: Difference Between Ones and Zeros in Row and Column

## 1. What the Difference Matrix Actually Measures

The input is a binary matrix `grid` with $m$ rows and $n$ columns, indexed from
zero. For every cell $(i, j)$ the task defines four counters:

- $\text{onesRow}_i$ — the number of ones in row $i$;
- $\text{onesCol}_j$ — the number of ones in column $j$;
- $\text{zerosRow}_i$ — the number of zeros in row $i$;
- $\text{zerosCol}_j$ — the number of zeros in column $j$.

The output cell is then

$$
\text{diff}[i][j] = \text{onesRow}_i + \text{onesCol}_j - \text{zerosRow}_i - \text{zerosCol}_j .
$$

The shape is unusual: the four terms do not all describe the same object. The
first and third are properties of row $i$ only, the second and fourth are
properties of column $j$ only, and no term looks at the individual bit
`grid[i][j]`. So the output is not a per-cell transform of the input; it is a
*rank-one additive combination* of a row profile and a column profile. That
distinction is what makes an $O(mn)$ time, $O(m + n)$ extra-space method — as
opposed to an $O(mn)$ scan per cell — the natural solution.

## 2. Collapsing Four Counters into Two Row Sums

Because `grid` is binary, the counts of zeros are determined by the counts of
ones. Along row $i$ there are exactly $n$ entries, so

$$
\text{zerosRow}_i = n - \text{onesRow}_i , \qquad
\text{zerosCol}_j = m - \text{onesCol}_j .
$$

Substituting removes the zero counters entirely:

$$
\text{diff}[i][j] = 2\,\text{onesRow}_i + 2\,\text{onesCol}_j - m - n .
$$

This is the form the lesson will reason about, and the form the reference
implementation's final expression encodes. It says that the entire output is
generated by two vectors — the row-sum vector $R = (R_0, \dots, R_{m-1})$ with
$R_i = \text{onesRow}_i$, and the column-sum vector $C = (C_0, \dots, C_{n-1})$
with $C_j = \text{onesCol}_j$ — plus the two constants $m$ and $n$.

A further simplification is available. Both $R$ and $C$ are projections of the
same matrix, so they share a single degree of freedom:

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{m-1} R_i \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} C_j \;=\; \#\{\text{ones in the whole grid}\} .
$$

The total number of ones is counted twice along different axes. This identity is
the invariant that ties the row profile to the column profile and lets the
analysis check itself: any computed $R$ and $C$ whose sums disagree signals a
bookkeeping error, not a property of the data.

## 3. The Bilinear Structure and Perfect Row Separation

Write the output as a function of two scalar arguments,

$$
D(r, c) = 2r + 2c - m - n ,
$$

so that $\text{diff}[i][j] = D(R_i, C_j)$. The function is affine and strictly
increasing in each argument, with partial derivatives $\partial D/\partial r = 2$
and $\partial D/\partial c = 2$. Two consequences follow, and both are visible in
every table below.

First, **within a fixed row $i$ the value varies only through $C_j$.** Moving
one column to the right changes the output by exactly $2(C_{j'} - C_j)$,
regardless of which row is being examined. The pattern of ups and downs across a
row is therefore identical in every row of the output; rows differ only by a
constant vertical shift of $2(R_{i'} - R_i)$.

Second, **the two profiles can be computed in a single pass over the matrix.**
Reading each entry once and adding it to $R_i$ and to $C_j$ produces both
vectors with $mn$ additions, after which the output costs $mn$ constant-time
evaluations of $D$. No cell is ever revisited, and no per-cell counting of row
or column populations is needed.

## 4. The Official Instance at a Glance

We trace the first official example, the mixed $3 \times 3$ grid, because it
contains all three interesting regimes: zero cells, positive cells, and negative
cells.

| Row $i$ | Row contents | $R_i = \text{onesRow}_i$ | $n - R_i = \text{zerosRow}_i$ |
|:---:|:---|:---:|:---:|
| 0 | `0 1 1` | 2 | 1 |
| 1 | `1 0 1` | 2 | 1 |
| 2 | `0 0 1` | 1 | 2 |

| Column $j$ | Column contents (top to bottom) | $C_j = \text{onesCol}_j$ | $m - C_j = \text{zerosCol}_j$ |
|:---:|:---|:---:|:---:|
| 0 | `0 1 0` | 1 | 2 |
| 1 | `0 1 0` | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | `1 1 1` | 3 | 0 |

Both profiles come from one pass. The row sums total $2 + 2 + 1 = 5$ and the
column sums total $1 + 1 + 3 = 5$, agreeing as the shared-ones invariant
requires: there are exactly five ones in the grid, and every row sum and every
column sum is a partition of that same five.

## 5. Complete Output Trace for `[[0,1,1],[1,0,1],[0,0,1]]`

Evaluating $D(R_i, C_j) = 2R_i + 2C_j - 3 - 3$ at each of the nine cells gives
the whole difference matrix. The table shows the two summands, the two
constants, and the result, so each entry can be checked by hand.

| Cell $(i,j)$ | $R_i$ | $C_j$ | $2R_i$ | $2C_j$ | $m + n$ | `diff[i][j]` |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $(0,0)$ | 2 | 1 | 4 | 2 | 6 | `0` |
| $(0,1)$ | 2 | 1 | 4 | 2 | 6 | `0` |
| $(0,2)$ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 6 | `4` |
| $(1,0)$ | 2 | 1 | 4 | 2 | 6 | `0` |
| $(1,1)$ | 2 | 1 | 4 | 2 | 6 | `0` |
| $(1,2)$ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 6 | `4` |
| $(2,0)$ | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 6 | `-2` |
| $(2,1)$ | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 6 | `-2` |
| $(2,2)$ | 1 | 3 | 2 | 6 | 6 | `2` |

Assembled into rows, this is

$$
\texttt{diff} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \\ -2 & -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix},
$$

which matches the authored expectation exactly. The perfect-row-separation
property from section 3 is on display: rows 0 and 1 have identical profiles
because $R_0 = R_1 = 2$, while row 2 is the same profile shifted down by
$2(R_1 - R_2) = 2$.

## 6. Reading the Sign of the Output

The sign of a cell has a clean interpretation. Since
$\text{diff}[i][j] = 2(R_i + C_j) - (m + n)$, the sign is decided by whether the
*combined* population of ones along row $i$ and column $j$ is more than half of
$m + n$. The boundary case $2(R_i + C_j) = m + n$ yields exactly `0`.

| Sign of `diff[i][j]` | Equivalent condition | Meaning for the instance above |
|:---:|:---|:---|
| positive | $R_i + C_j > \dfrac{m + n}{2} = 3$ | column 2 is dense with ones ($C_2 = 3$), so cells $(0,2)$ and $(1,2)$ are positive |
| zero | $R_i + C_j = 3$ | rows 0 and 1 combined with columns 0 and 1; $(1,1)$ sits on a one but the row/column totals are balanced |
| negative | $R_i + C_j < 3$ | row 2 is one-sparse ($R_2 = 1$) and columns 0, 1 are one-sparse too, so $(2,0)$ and $(2,1)$ are negative |

The row-1 entry $(1,1)$ is the instructive one: `grid[1][1]` is `0`, yet the
output is `0` rather than negative, because $R_1$ and $C_1$ are large enough to
offset the constant. Conversely `grid[2][2]` is `1` and the output is `2`, but
`grid[0][2]` is also `1` with output `4`. The lesson is that the bit at
`grid[i][j]` does not determine the sign or the magnitude of
`diff[i][j]`; only the two marginal profiles do.

## 7. The One Pass That Builds Both Profiles

The state maintained by the method is small enough to display directly, and this
is the per-step trace of the accumulation. It shows each entry of the grid being
charged to exactly one row counter and one column counter.

| Step | Entry read | Row counter updated | Column counter updated | Row vector $R$ | Column vector $C$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| initial | — | — | — | `[0, 0, 0]` | `[0, 0, 0]` |
| 1 | `grid[0][0] = 0` | $R_0$ unchanged | $C_0$ unchanged | `[0, 0, 0]` | `[0, 0, 0]` |
| 2 | `grid[0][1] = 1` | $R_0 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | $C_1 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | `[1, 0, 0]` | `[0, 1, 0]` |
| 3 | `grid[0][2] = 1` | $R_0 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | $C_2 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | `[2, 0, 0]` | `[0, 1, 1]` |
| 4 | `grid[1][0] = 1` | $R_1 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | $C_0 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | `[2, 1, 0]` | `[1, 1, 1]` |
| 5 | `grid[1][1] = 0` | $R_1$ unchanged | $C_1$ unchanged | `[2, 1, 0]` | `[1, 1, 1]` |
| 6 | `grid[1][2] = 1` | $R_1 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | $C_2 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | `[2, 2, 0]` | `[1, 1, 2]` |
| 7 | `grid[2][0] = 0` | $R_2$ unchanged | $C_0$ unchanged | `[2, 2, 0]` | `[1, 1, 2]` |
| 8 | `grid[2][1] = 0` | $R_2$ unchanged | $C_1$ unchanged | `[2, 2, 0]` | `[1, 1, 2]` |
| 9 | `grid[2][2] = 1` | $R_2 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | $C_2 \mathrel{+}= 1$ | `[2, 2, 1]` | `[1, 1, 3]` |

After step 9 both vectors agree with the profiles derived in section 4. Notice
that zeros contribute nothing to either counter — they are invisible to the
accumulation and only re-enter through the constants $m$ and $n$ in $D$. This is
why the zero-complement substitution of section 2 is not merely a convenience:
the algorithm genuinely never needs to count zeros.

## 8. Why the Formula Is Correct, Cell by Cell

The construction is correct because of a local identity that holds at every
cell, and the identity is worth stating explicitly rather than trusting the
algebra. Fix $(i, j)$ and ask how many times the bit `grid[i][j]` is counted by
each of the four terms:

| Term | Includes the bit `grid[i][j]`? | Contribution of that bit to the term |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| $\text{onesRow}_i$ | yes | `grid[i][j]` |
| $\text{onesCol}_j$ | yes | `grid[i][j]` |
| $\text{zerosRow}_i$ | yes, as its complement | $1 - \texttt{grid[i][j]}$ |
| $\text{zerosCol}_j$ | yes, as its complement | $1 - \texttt{grid[i][j]}$ |

Summing with the problem's signs gives a net coefficient of
`grid[i][j] + grid[i][j] - (1 - grid[i][j]) - (1 - grid[i][j]) = 4*grid[i][j] - 2`
for the bit itself, so the term $\text{diff}[i][j]$ contains
$4\,\texttt{grid[i][j]} - 2$ attributable to that bit.

That coefficient is a striking fact with a clean reading: a one at `grid[i][j]`
pushes the output up by $+2$ and a zero pushes it down by $-2$, a swing of $4$
for a single flipped bit. Now every *other* entry of the grid is counted
independently of its position: a one elsewhere contributes $+1$ for each time it
lies in row $i$ or column $j$ and $-1$ for each time it does not. The
accumulated baseline over the rest of the grid is exactly $2(R_i + C_j) -
4\,\texttt{grid[i][j]}$, and adding the bit's own contribution recovers
$2(R_i + C_j) - 2$, that is, $D(R_i, C_j)$ once $m + n$ is absorbed. The formula
is therefore not an approximation but an exact expansion, valid for every binary
matrix.

A subtlety worth naming: the signed sum of all four counters counts each entry
of the matrix exactly twice with sign $+1$ and twice with sign $-1$, so it is
*not* a conservation law — the result is not constant across cells. The
constants $m$ and $n$ are what make the combination meaningful, and they always
appear together as the pair sum $m + n$.

## 9. Boundary and Degenerate Instances

The constraints $1 \le m, n \le 10^5$ with $1 \le mn \le 10^5$ admit several
degenerate shapes, and the table records how the same two-vector method behaves
on each. The values are checked against the package's authored expectations.

| Instance | $m, n$ | Profiles | Why it is a worthwhile trap | Output |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|:---|
| `[[0]]` | $1, 1$ | $R = [0]$, $C = [0]$ | the single cell is a zero, so both populations are empty and $D = -2$ | `[[-2]]` |
| `[[1]]` | $1, 1$ | $R = [1]$, $C = [1]$ | the all-one unit cell is the opposite extreme, $D = +2$ | `[[2]]` |
| `[[0,0,0],[0,0,0]]` | $2, 3$ | $R = [0,0]$, $C = [0,0,0]$ | all-zeros grid: $D$ is constant at $-(m+n) = -5$, never $0$ | `[[-5,-5,-5],[-5,-5,-5]]` |
| `[[1,1,1],[1,1,1]]` | $2, 3$ | $R = [3,3]$, $C = [2,2,2]$ | all-ones grid: $D = m + n = 5$ everywhere, the maximum for this shape | `[[5,5,5],[5,5,5]]` |
| `[[1,0],[0,1]]` | $2, 2$ | $R = [1,1]$, $C = [1,1]$ | the diagonal ones cancel: $D = 0$ at all four cells although the grid is not uniform | `[[0,0],[0,0]]` |
| `[[1,0,1,0]]` | $1, 4$ | $R = [2]$, $C = [1,0,1,0]$ | a single row: the output varies *only* with $C_j$, alternating $+1$ and $-1$ | `[[1,-1,1,-1]]` |
| `[[1],[0],[1]]` | $3, 1$ | $R = [1,0,1]$, $C = [2]$ | a single column: the output varies *only* with $R_i$, giving `2`, `0`, `2` | `[[2],[0],[2]]` |

Two traps stand out. The diagonal instance `[[1,0],[0,1]]` shows that a
non-uniform grid can produce an all-zero output when $R_i + C_j = (m+n)/2$ holds
everywhere — a whole matrix of zeros is not evidence that the input was empty.
The all-zeros instance shows the opposite trap: a matrix containing no ones at
all produces the constant $-(m+n)$, which for $m = n = 1$ is $-2$, so an
implementation that shortcuts "no ones means all zeros" is simply wrong.

## 10. Alternative Strategies and Their Trade-offs

| Strategy | Extra state | Time beyond the output | Why it loses or when it is appropriate |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Direct quadruple counting | none | $O(mn \cdot (m + n))$ if each cell rescans its row and column, or $O(mn)$ with four precomputed counter arrays | Correct but carries four arrays; the zero-complement substitution halves the memory with no loss. |
| Store both ones and zeros counters | $O(m + n)$ | $O(mn)$ | Fully faithful to the statement's four definitions, but two of the four arrays are redundant by $n - R_i$ and $m - C_j$. |
| Two profiles plus the closed form | $O(m + n)$ | $O(mn)$ | The chosen method: one accumulation pass, then one evaluation per output cell. |
| Row-difference propagation | $O(m + n)$ | $O(mn)$ | Compute the output for column 0 of each row, then add the constant step $2(C_j - C_{j-1})$ along the row. Saves evaluations but complicates indexing for no asymptotic gain. |

## 11. Complexity

Let $m$ be the number of rows and $n$ the number of columns, so the grid holds
$mn$ entries and the constraints guarantee $mn \le 10^5$.

- **Time:** $O(mn)$, i.e. $\Theta(mn)$. The accumulation pass reads each entry
  exactly once and performs two additions for it; the output pass performs a
  fixed number of arithmetic operations per cell. The output itself has $mn$
  entries, so no correct method can do less than $\Omega(mn)$ — this bound is
  optimal, not merely an upper bound.
- **Auxiliary space:** $O(m + n)$ beyond the returned matrix, for the row-sum
  vector of length $m$ and the column-sum vector of length $n$. The returned
  $m \times n$ matrix is the required output and is not auxiliary. Working in
  place is not possible without destroying the input, since the output of cell
  $(i, j)$ depends on the row and column totals of the *original* grid.

The key efficiency claim is that the four counters of the definition never need
to be materialised: two of them are complements of the other two, so the method
trades $O(m + n)$ registers for the entire zero-counting dimension of the
problem.
