## General

**Use pairs of boundary darts to generate candidate circles.** The dartboard radius is fixed. If two darts are farther than `2r` apart, no radius-`r` circle can contain both because the diameter is the greatest possible distance between two points in such a circle. If their distance is at most `2r`, there are one or two circle centers whose boundary passes through both points.

The helper `possibleCenters(x1, y1, x2, y2)` computes those centers. Let the vector from the first dart to the second be `(dx, dy)` and its length be `d`. The center of any circle through both points must be equally distant from them, so it lies on their perpendicular bisector.

The midpoint `(mid_x, mid_y)` is the base point on that bisector. Drawing a segment from a candidate center to either dart forms a right triangle. Its hypotenuse is `r`, one leg from the midpoint to a dart has length `d / 2`, and the other leg from the midpoint to the center has length
`sqrt(r*r - (d/2)*(d/2))`.

A unit vector perpendicular to `(dx, dy)` is `(dy/d, -dx/d)`. Multiplying it by that center distance gives offsets `offset_x` and `offset_y`. Adding and subtracting those offsets from the midpoint produces the two symmetric candidate centers on opposite sides of the chord.

If `d == 2r`, the perpendicular distance is zero and the two returned tuples are identical. Counting that same center twice affects running time only; taking a maximum leaves the result unchanged. The darts are guaranteed unique, so `d` is never zero and division by `d` is safe.

**Reject pairs that cannot share the board.** When `d > 2r`, `possibleCenters` returns an empty list. The outer code then has no center to test for that pair. This prevents taking the square root of a negative value and represents the geometric impossibility directly.

**Count all darts for each candidate center.** `countDarts(x, y)` scans every input point and measures its Euclidean distance to the proposed center using `dist`. A dart belongs on the board when that distance is no more than the radius. Points exactly on the circular boundary count as inside.

Floating-point calculations can produce a tiny value above `r` for a point that is mathematically on the boundary. The comparison allows `r + 1e-7` to absorb ordinary rounding error. The tolerance is deliberately small relative to the coordinate scale; it should correct numerical noise rather than materially enlarge the board.

The main loops consider every unordered pair `i < j`. For every feasible pair, they test both centers and update `max_darts` with the number contained. Starting `max_darts` at one handles the case of a single dart and also guarantees a valid baseline: a circle of positive radius can always be centered on at least one dart.

**Why pair-derived centers are sufficient.** Suppose an optimal board contains at least two darts. There exists an equally good placement of the same fixed-radius circle in which at least two contained darts lie on its boundary. Intuitively, move the center while maintaining containment until one dart becomes tight, then move along that tight dart's radius constraint until another becomes tight. At such an extremal feasible placement, those two boundary darts determine one of the centers generated by `possibleCenters`.

Therefore, testing all centers defined by feasible dart pairs reaches a circle containing the optimal set. If the best count is only one, the initial value already covers it. The algorithm does not need to try arbitrary real-valued centers across the plane.

**A geometric example.** Take darts at `(-2, 0)` and `(2, 0)` with radius two. Their distance is four, exactly the diameter. The midpoint is `(0, 0)` and the perpendicular distance is zero, so the only geometric center is the origin. Counting from that center also includes darts at `(0, 2)` and `(0, -2)`, giving four.

For two closer darts, the perpendicular distance is positive. Both centers must be tested because other darts may lie predominantly on one side of the chord. Choosing only one sign could miss the placement containing the most points.

**Why each counted result is valid.** Every generated center is constructed at distance `r` from its defining pair, up to floating-point tolerance. `countDarts` includes only points whose center distance is at most the allowed radius plus the numerical epsilon. Thus each candidate count corresponds to a realizable radius-`r` board placement.

The maximum over all candidate placements cannot exceed the true optimum because every candidate is a legal placement. The extremal-center argument shows that some enumerated placement reaches the true optimum, so the maximum cannot be smaller either.

**Be precise about the stored source's complexity.** The manifest advertises `O(n^2 log n)` time, which is the common angular-sweep solution. The exact stored code does something different: it generates `O(n^2)` pair centers and calls an `O(n)` full counting scan for each. Its actual running time is `O(n^3)`. It is still correct for the small limit of one hundred darts, but its complexity should be derived from these nested operations rather than from the manifest label.

## Complexity detail

Let `n` be the number of darts. There are `n(n - 1) / 2` unordered pairs. Each pair produces at most two centers using constant-time arithmetic. For each center, `countDarts` scans all `n` points. The exact implementation therefore takes `O(n^3)` time.

The helper lists contain at most two centers, and counting uses scalar variables only. Excluding the input, auxiliary space is `O(1)`. The nested loops do not store all pairs or all centers.

The manifest's `O(n^2 log n)` time and `O(n)` space correspond to fixing each dart as an anchor, converting every other dart's feasible center positions into angular intervals, and sorting interval endpoints. That is a stronger algorithm but is not the code in this source file.

Distance and square-root operations are treated as constant time in the usual geometric model. Floating-point precision affects robustness, not the asymptotic count of operations.

## Alternatives and edge cases

- **Angular sweep around each anchor:** For each dart, compute the angular interval of centers at distance `r` that would also contain every other reachable dart, sort interval events, and find maximum overlap. This achieves the manifest's `O(n^2 log n)` time and `O(n)` space but requires careful wraparound and event ordering.
- **Pair centers with squared-distance counting:** Compare squared distances with `r*r` to avoid a square root for every counted dart. Center coordinates are still floating-point, but this improves constants without changing the cubic bound.
- **Try centers at dart positions only:** This is incorrect. An optimal circle's center need not coincide with any dart.
- **Generate only one center per pair:** This can miss a better placement on the other side of the chord. Both perpendicular signs matter.
- **Single dart:** No pair loop runs, and the initialized answer one is correct.
- **Pair farther than the diameter:** No fixed-radius circle contains both, so it generates no centers.
- **Pair exactly one diameter apart:** The two formulas coincide at the midpoint. Duplicate evaluation does not change the maximum.
- **Very close distinct darts:** The division by `d` is valid because points are unique, though floating-point care is important when `d` is small.
- **Dart on the boundary:** It counts as inside. The small epsilon protects this inclusive rule from rounding error.
- **All darts fit one circle:** Some pair-derived extremal center reaches all of them, and counting returns `n`.
- **Collinear darts:** The same chord geometry applies. Feasible pair centers lie on perpendicular lines, and the maximum is still found.
- **Negative coordinates:** Distances use coordinate differences and squares, so signs require no special handling.
- **Duplicate darts outside the contract:** `d = 0` would cause division by zero in `possibleCenters`. The uniqueness guarantee is essential to this exact implementation.
- **Floating comparison at d versus 2r:** The early rejection has no epsilon. Integer-coordinate distance calculations are usually stable here, but a defensive geometric implementation may compare squared distances with a tolerance.
- **Tolerance too large:** A generous epsilon could count points truly outside the board. The selected `1e-7` is intended only for floating rounding.
- **Complexity reporting:** Report `O(n^3)` time and `O(1)` auxiliary space for this exact source. Reserve `O(n^2 log n)` and `O(n)` for an implemented angular sweep.
